The message usually shows up on a Tuesday.

"Small thing. Can we also add a way to export this?"

Small thing. Also. Two words that have screwed more launch dates than any bug ever will.

You say yes, because it is a small thing. Then sales wants a filter. Then the VP who skipped the kickoff wants a dashboard view. Then you notice the empty state looks rough and fix it too, because you're a designer and you can't help yourself.

Nobody decided to blow the deadline. It happened one reasonable yes at a time.

That's scope creep. Requirements that grow after the work has started, while the timeline and the team stay exactly the same size. And in product design it hits harder than almost anywhere else.

## Why scope creep hits product designers hardest

Three reasons, and you'll recognize all of them.

**Design is the first stop for every new idea.** Nobody asks engineering to add a dashboard on a Tuesday. They ask the designer to "just mock it up." Just. As if your time is free and the sprint is made of rubber. By the time an idea reaches a developer, it's already a frame in your file, and a frame in your file looks like a commitment.

**Changing a design looks free.** Moving a button in Figma takes ten seconds, so people assume the change costs ten seconds. It doesn't. Every change ripples into states, edge cases, copy, QA and the developer who already built the old version. The cost shows up later, on someone else's calendar, which is exactly why nobody sees it coming.

**AI made it worse.** When you can [prototype ten ideas in an afternoon](/letters/ai-prototyping-workflow/), every one of them starts to feel shippable. Your PM sees a variant and wants it. You see a better pattern and want it. Speed made options cheap. Shipping them costs exactly what it always did. I build a lot in [Cursor instead of Figma](/letters/cursor-for-designers/), and the faster my tools got, the more discipline I needed about what actually goes out the door.

## What scope creep looks like in product design

Most advice treats scope creep like a single problem. In design work it shows up as four different ones, and each needs a different response.

| Type | What it sounds like | What's really happening |
|---|---|---|
| The also | "Can we also add export?" | A new feature riding along on an approved one |
| The drive-by | "I saw the designs. Quick thought." | A stakeholder who skipped the kickoff, reopening it |
| The polish spiral | Nobody says anything. You just keep going. | You, gold-plating details nobody asked for |
| The real discovery | "Users are actually stuck on step two." | New information that changes the problem |

The first three are creep. The fourth is learning, and you should act on it. More on that below.

Notice the third one. A lot of scope creep in design is self-inflicted. You find a better pattern halfway through, or you can't stand shipping a rough edge, and suddenly the two-week project is in week four. Same damage. And you can't even blame the VP for this one. That's on you.

## What one small also actually costs

Let's put a number on it, because "it's just a small thing" survives right up until somebody does the math.

Say you've got a two-week project. Ten working days. Three requests come in along the way:

- Export: two days of design, states and dev handoff
- A filter for sales: one and a half days
- Your own empty state cleanup: one day, if you're honest

That's four and a half days. Nearly half the project, and not one person in the room said yes to a 45% longer timeline. They each said yes to one small thing.

And it compounds. The launch slips, so the metric you promised moves later, so the [ROI story for the work](/letters/roi-of-design/) gets weaker, so the next time you ask for time on a project, you get less of it. Scope creep eats this project, then it eats your credibility on the next one.

These are illustrative numbers. Run your own on the last project that slipped. I'd bet a coffee the pattern holds.

## The swap rule: the deadline is fixed, scope is the variable

Here's the rule I run every project on.

**Time is fixed. Scope is what moves.**

You agree on the time budget up front. One sprint, two sprints, six weeks. That number doesn't change when a new idea shows up. So every new request becomes a swap, never an add.

Something comes in. Something else goes out. The person who asked decides what.

<!-- figure:scope-swap -->

This does three things:

1. **It turns a yes or no into a choice.** Nobody's blocked. They're holding a tradeoff now.
2. **It puts the cost on the table.** "Can we also" is free when nobody names the price. A swap makes the price visible.
3. **It keeps you off the hook.** Nobody remembers you as the designer who blocked the VP. They remember you asked which of two things mattered more.

Most stakeholders, once they see the trade, pick the original plan. The new idea goes on the list for next time, and nobody's annoyed.

The swap rule only works if the time budget and the scope were written down at the start. If your kickoff ended with "cool, we're aligned," you have nothing to swap against. I broke down [how to run a project kickoff meeting](/letters/project-kickoff-meeting/) that forces those numbers onto the page.

## What to say when the next request lands

Here's the part nobody teaches. The actual goddamn words. One script per situation, so pick the tab that matches the mess you're in:

- **The also:** don't say no. Say what it costs.
- **The drive-by:** bring them back to what was already decided, out loud.
- **The polish spiral:** a conversation with yourself.
- **The exec:** make the cost public and hand them the decision.
- **The client:** same swap, except the trade has a price tag in dollars.

```text also.txt
Love it. We're full for this cycle, so if export goes in, something comes out.
Right now the options are the bulk edit flow or the onboarding tooltip.
Which one would you rather drop? Or we park export for the next cycle.
```
```text drive-by.txt
Good catch. Quick context: in the kickoff we agreed this cycle is about
getting new admins through setup, and dashboards were on the out list.
Do you want to reopen that? If so, let's do it with Sam in the room,
since it changes the timeline.
```
```text polish.txt
Does this change move the metric we agreed on?
If no: write it down for later and stop touching it.
If yes: it's a swap. What comes out?
```
```text exec.txt
Happy to add it. To be clear on the trade: adding the dashboard moves launch
from the 14th to the 28th, or we cut bulk edit to hold the date.
Your call. I'll update the plan and send it to the team either way.
```
```text client.txt
Happy to add the export flow. It's outside the scope we signed, so here are
two options: I swap it in for the reporting screen at no extra cost,
or I add it as a change order at $1,200 and two extra days. Which works better?
```

Freelancers get eaten alive by scope creep because every unpaid also comes straight out of your hourly rate. A change order is a swap with an invoice attached. If you're absorbing extras to keep a client happy, you're giving yourself a quiet pay cut. Check [what product designers actually earn](/letters/product-designer-salary/) and price your time like you mean it.

Every script follows the same shape. Acknowledge the idea, name the trade, hand back the decision, write it down. You never argue about whether the idea is good. It might be great. Great ideas still cost time, and nobody gets a free sprint.

## Keep a change log, and make it boring

Scripts handle the moment. The change log handles the memory.

Three weeks from now, someone will ask why export didn't ship. If the answer lives in a Slack thread, you'll lose that argument, and honestly, you'll deserve to. If it lives in a one-line entry with a name and a date, you won't.

Keep it in the same doc as the plan. Ideally that's your PRD, under the out-of-scope list. Here's [the PRD template I use](/letters/prd-template/) if you need one.

```markdown scope-change-log.md
## Scope changes

| Date | Request | Asked by | Decision | Trade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 14 | CSV export | Priya (Sales) | Parked, next cycle | None |
| Oct 16 | Dashboard view | Marc (VP) | In | Bulk edit cut |
| Oct 20 | Fix empty states | Me | In | Took from buffer day |
```

Boring is the point. Boring wins arguments. A boring log means every change was a decision, with a name on it. No surprises at the retro.

It also makes your case study easier later. "We cut bulk edit to ship the dashboard, and here's why" is exactly the kind of tradeoff hiring managers look for. There's a section for it in my [UX case study template](/letters/ux-case-study-template/), and I wrote about how to [explain design decisions without defending them](/letters/design-rationale/). The log is where both start.

## When saying yes is the right call

Some scope changes are good news.

If you learn something mid-project that changes the problem, ignoring it is worse than creep. Users stuck on step two when you're polishing step four is a reason to change course.

The test is simple. **Does this change the problem, or just add to the solution?**

<!-- figure:scope-triage -->

- **New information about the problem:** stop and re-bet. Pull the team together, agree on what changed, and reset the scope against the same time budget.
- **More ideas for the solution:** swap it in or park it.

The re-bet matters. A quiet pivot that only the designer knows about is still scope creep. It just has a nicer haircut. If the bet changed, the success metric probably did too. Check it against the [UX metrics that actually make it into the exec deck](/letters/ux-metrics/).

## Stop it before the project starts

Every script above works better when there's a written agreement to point back to. The time budget. The problem. The out list. A metric everyone said out loud.

Without that, there was never a scope to creep. Just a free-for-all with a Jira board.

Half the time the creep was baked in before you even got the project. The brief showed up pre-decided, with a solution already picked and nobody asking what it was worth. I wrote about [how to write a product brief that doesn't arrive half-wrong](/letters/product-brief-template/), and if you're fuzzy on what belongs in the doc that comes after it, start with [what a PRD actually is](/letters/what-is-a-prd/).

That's also why I run [Builder Kickoff™](/builder-kickoff/) before anyone opens Figma. The team commits the appetite first, the time the problem is worth, then maps the current flow and sorts every idea into in, maybe or out. Scope gets argued once, in the room, instead of one Slack message at a time.

## Scope creep FAQ

### What is scope creep in product design?

Scope creep is when a project's requirements grow after work has started, without the timeline or team growing to match. In product design it often arrives as small asks to "also" add something, feedback from stakeholders who missed the kickoff, or designers polishing past what was agreed.

### What causes scope creep in design projects?

Usually three things: no written time budget or out-of-scope list at the start, stakeholders who skipped the kickoff and reopen decisions later, and design changes that look cheap in Figma but cost real time downstream. AI prototyping tools add a fourth, because generating more options makes more of them feel shippable.

### How do you handle scope creep with stakeholders?

Keep the deadline fixed and treat every new request as a swap. Acknowledge the idea, name what would have to come out to make room, and let the person who asked choose. Then log the decision with their name and the date.

### How do you say no to a stakeholder without damaging the relationship?

Don't say no. Say what it costs. "If this goes in, bulk edit comes out. Which would you rather ship?" turns a rejection into a choice they control, and most people pick the original plan once they see the trade.

### How should freelance designers handle scope creep?

Treat anything outside the signed scope as a swap or a paid change order. Offer the client both options in writing, with the cost in days and dollars, and let them pick. Absorbing extras for free comes straight out of your effective hourly rate.

### Is scope creep always bad?

No. If new information changes the problem itself, adjusting is the right call. The difference is whether you re-bet out loud with the team, resetting scope against the same time budget, or let the project quietly grow.

### How do you prevent scope creep before it starts?

Agree on three things in the kickoff and write them down: how much time the problem is worth, what's out of scope, and the metric that defines success. Those give you something to point back to when the first also arrives.
