Someone I coach messaged me a few weeks ago.

Fewer design jobs out there, she said. And the ones that exist want designers who act more like PMs or engineers. Would her skills still fit?

She's right about the market.

I also think it's the best news designers have had in years.

Stick with me. By the end you'll have the loop I run on every project, and the math that gets a designer taken seriously in a room full of PMs.

## Building got cheap

I haven't opened Figma in two and a half months. Claude builds my prototypes now. Real screens, real data, stuff you can click. ([Here's how that went](/letters/designing-without-figma/), including the parts that sucked.)

So the expensive part of the job moved.

It used to be making the thing. Now it's picking the thing. And three questions got very expensive, very fast:

1. What do we build?
2. What do we leave alone?
3. Sixty days after launch, did it actually work?

Those used to be the PM's problem. They're yours now too. Your team can ship the wrong feature in a week, and nobody's going to stop you because it looks great in the demo.

Quick gut check. Say, in one sentence, what your current project is supposed to move and by how much.

Can't? Then nobody on your team can either. You're all building on vibes and hoping the launch post gets enough likes.

The designer who can say "that one, not that one, and here's the number it'll move" is the one who gets hired. And the one who gets [pulled into the room early](/letters/product-designer-portfolio/).

So what's everyone doing instead? Racing to learn the tools. Which is exactly the wrong race.

## The AI part is the easy part

Every job post wants an "AI-native designer" now.

I think most of them are hiring for the wrong thing.

AI is the easiest part of my workflow. It builds my prototypes. It drafts the brief after a kickoff. It reminds me when a feature has been live for 60 days. Any decent designer can pick that up in a month.

What nobody picks up in a month is aiming at what you're shooting at.

Getting product, engineering and design to agree on what something is worth. Deciding what you're not building. Writing down the number you expect to move before anyone opens a tool. Then going back 60 days later and saying "this didn't work" out loud, in front of the people who asked for it.

If your pitch is "I'm fluent in eight AI tools," you're competing with every designer who watched the same YouTube video.

If your pitch is "I know what we're shooting at and I can prove we hit it," you're competing with almost nobody.

So how do you actually learn to aim? For me it started with one question I couldn't stop asking.

## Where this started

Two years ago I had one question. How the hell do I design something I'm proud of without watching it turn into a half-assed version of itself three weeks later?

You know the pattern.

Nobody's in the room at the same time. Nobody agrees on how much time the thing gets. Design draws the dream version. Then engineering finds the constraints, the PM finds the deadline, and the thing you designed dies [one "can we also" at a time](/letters/scope-creep/) in a ticket thread. Nobody decided to ship the shitty version. It just happened, one comment at a time.

That question turned into [Builder Kickoff](/builder-kickoff/?utm_source=site&utm_medium=letter&utm_campaign=builder-loop). One session, everyone in the room at the same time, before anyone opens a design tool.

The order matters more than the agenda:

1. **Appetite first.** How many weeks is this worth? Agree on that before anyone talks solutions, and let that number decide the scope.
2. **Engineering talks constraints first.** Before design shows a single sketch. If the engineering lead can't make it, reschedule. I'm dead serious. Without them you're writing a wish list and calling it a plan.
3. **Scope call last.** You walk out with one decision sentence everybody agreed to out loud.

And three lines I use to keep the room honest:

- "We're not here to collect opinions. We're here to leave with a buildable decision."
- "If we include this, what gets cut?"
- "If someone asks tomorrow what we decided, what's the one-sentence answer?"

You still get friction. It's the useful kind. The kind that happens before anyone's attached to a mockup.

The first time I ran it as a proper 90-minute session, I designed the full feature end to end the next day. Presented it to everyone the day after. PMs and engineers included. Zero pushback.

That's the extreme case. On most projects it just kills a ton of the friction you'd otherwise eat later. (The full agenda is [here](/letters/project-kickoff-meeting/) if you want to steal it.)

Look at step one on that list again. Appetite. It's the one everybody skips, because it sounds like the softest thing in the room. It's the opposite.

## Appetite is a money question

"Appetite" sounds soft. Like a vibe about how hungry the team is.

It's the hardest number in the room.

Appetite is how many weeks this thing is worth, decided before anyone estimates how long it'll take. And the fastest way to get an honest answer is to price the room.

Count everyone who'll touch it. Design, PM, every engineer, QA. Then put a cost on a week of their time.

Don't use anyone's real salary. Nobody needs to know what the backend lead makes, and HR will hunt you down. Use a blended number. Ask finance for one, or take a market salary, multiply by 1.3 for benefits and overhead, and divide by 52.

Here's the math with made-up but realistic numbers:

- Five people on it. Blended salary $150k.
- $150k &times; 1.3 &divide; 52 = about $3,750 a week each.
- The room costs $18,750 a week.
- A six-week appetite is $112,500.

<!-- figure:appetite-cost -->

Now flip the question. What does this feature have to make or save to pay that back?

**Break-even** is the number where it pays for itself. If an average account is worth $15k a year, you need about eight new or saved accounts in year one just to get your money back.

**Revenue** is everything above that line. That's the number that goes in the decision sentence, and it's the number you check at day 60.

If nobody in the room believes this can land eight accounts, the appetite's wrong. Cut it to three weeks ($56k, four accounts), or don't build the damn thing.

The first time you do this out loud, it's awkward as hell. Do it anyway. Nothing kills a bad feature faster than a price tag. And it's exactly the kind of math that turns "the designer" into [someone the business listens to](/letters/roi-of-design/).

But the kickoff was only the start. The part that kept bugging the shit out of me was the end.

## "We'll ship and learn"

You've heard it. Let's just ship it and learn.

I'm not against that. Build fast, launch, iterate. Great.

But here's what actually happens. The iteration cycle is so long it never comes. The feature ships, everyone moves on, and the learning part dies without anyone noticing. Sixty days later somebody asks what it did, and the honest answer is "no fucking idea, but people seemed to like it."

That's a vibe with a launch date.

And it's exactly how design's value stays invisible. You did the work and you have zero receipts. Good luck putting "people seemed to like it" in a [case study](/letters/ux-case-study-template/).

So when someone says "ship and learn," I ask one question. Is learning actually part of our process? Are we looking at the [analytics](/letters/ux-metrics/)? Talking to sales? Going back to users?

Or are we just saying it to get the damn thing out the door?

If you want it to be real, set it up before launch, while everyone still cares:

- **Book the readout the day you ship.** Sixty days out, PM and engineering lead invited. If it's not on a calendar, it doesn't exist.
- **Name who pulls the number.** Before launch. "Someone will check the dashboard" means nobody will.
- **Bring more than a chart.** One note from a sales call and one quote from a user, next to the number. The chart tells you what moved. The people tell you why.

<!-- figure:ship-and-learn -->

Those three fix the ending. But one good readout won't save a team that skipped the beginning. You need the whole thing, start to finish, on repeat.

## The loop

This is what I run now, start to finish. I call it the Builder Loop.

1. **Find the target.** One appetite, three non-goals, one decision sentence: we will ship X for Y by Z, without A, B or C.
2. **Write it down.** A [decision brief](/letters/product-brief-template/) everyone builds against, so the target doesn't drift one "quick question" at a time.
3. **Prove it's worth hitting.** Two real insights with a source, and the number you expect to move. Written down before design starts.
4. **Build toward it.** A working prototype in five days, checked against the decision sentence every single day.
5. **Check your aim.** A quality pass before handoff, and visual QA before you call it done.
6. **Did you hit it?** Sixty days after real customers can use it, predicted next to actual. Then one call: keep, iterate, investigate or stop.

Stage six feeds the next stage one. What you learned becomes the next target. That's why it's a loop.

<!-- figure:loop-ring -->

Shipping is the halfway point. Most teams stop there and call it a win.

So where does your team fall off the loop? There's a six-question test for that. It takes about two minutes, and most teams fail it by question three.

## Where your team loses the target

Grab the last thing your team shipped. Pick the one nobody's mentioned since launch day.

Now ask these in order. One per stage of the loop:

1. What were we building, for who, and what were we not building?
2. Where's the doc that says why we made each call?
3. What number was it supposed to move, and where was that number on day one?
4. Did we see it working before we committed a full build to it?
5. Does what shipped match what we decided?
6. Sixty days later, did the number move?

If the answer to number two is "it's in a Slack thread somewhere," that's a no. If the answer to number three is a shrug, that's a hell no.

The first one nobody can answer is where your team loses the target.

For most teams it's number three. Nobody wrote the number down, so nobody can ever check it. Which means nobody can ever claim the win either, including you.

The good news? Number three is the cheapest one on the list to fix. It takes fifteen minutes and one slightly awkward question.

## Try it on your next project

I saw why this matters on a page redesign. The room had already agreed on what the problem was. Then we opened Mixpanel, and the data pointed somewhere completely different. If we'd gone with the room, we would have shipped a beautiful fix for the wrong problem.

Qual and quant speak louder than the voices in the room.

So before anyone opens Figma, write four lines at the top of the brief:

1. **The decision:** We will ship X for Y by Z.
2. **The non-goals:** Three things we're not building this cycle.
3. **The number:** We expect [metric] to go from [baseline] to [target], measured in [source].
4. **The date:** 60 days after real customers can use it, we check.

It takes about 15 minutes. Put that date in your calendar right now, while you still care.

If line three is hard to fill in, that's the most useful thing you'll learn all week. No baseline means you instrument first. Ask engineering to add the tracking as part of the build, and write "none yet" instead of guessing.

Then you wait sixty days. And then comes the meeting most teams quietly chicken out of.

## What the day-60 call sounds like

This is the meeting where careers get made. Most designers skip it, because the answer might be "it didn't work" and nobody wants to say that with the VP in the room.

Show up anyway. The designer who says "we were wrong, here's why, here's what we do next" is the one who gets trusted with the next big bet.

When the date comes up, put what you predicted next to what actually happened, with the source and the date. Then make one call:

- **Keep.** It hit the target. Leave it alone and stop spending on it.
- **Iterate.** It moved, but not enough. Run another loop on it, with what you learned as the new target.
- **Investigate.** Something weird happened. The number moved the wrong way, or something else moved instead. Find out why before you decide anything.
- **Stop.** It didn't work. Pull it, or at least stop pouring money into it. Sunk cost is a hell of a drug.

Then write one more line: what would change your mind about this call? It keeps you honest, and it tells the next loop exactly what to look for.

In the playbook's example, the team expected new workspaces with two or more members to go from 18% to 30%. At day 60 it was 26%. The call was iterate, because most failed invites came from one gap the team had already spotted while building. That gap became the next target. That's the loop closing.
