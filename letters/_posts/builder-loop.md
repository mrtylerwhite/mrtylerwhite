Someone I coach messaged me a few weeks ago.

Fewer design jobs out there, she said. And the ones that exist want designers who act more like PMs or engineers. Would her skills still fit?

She's right about the market.

I also think it's the best news designers have had in years.

## Building got cheap

The new tools build a working screen before your coffee's cold. I spent two and a half months designing this site without opening Figma ([here's how that went](/letters/designing-without-figma/)). Making the thing is fast now. Stupid fast.

So the expensive part moved.

It used to be making the thing. Now it's picking the thing. What do we build? What do we leave alone? And sixty days after launch, did it actually work?

That used to be the PM's question. It's yours now too.

Teams can ship the wrong feature faster than ever. The designer who can say "that one, not that one, and here's the number it'll move" is the one who gets hired. And the one who gets [pulled into the room early](/letters/product-designer-portfolio/).

## Where this started

Two years ago I had one question. How do I design something I'm proud of that doesn't get hacked to pieces three weeks later?

You know the pattern.

Nobody's in the room at the same time. Nobody agrees on how much time the thing gets. Design draws the dream version. Then engineering finds the constraints, the PM finds the deadline, and your dream gets gutted [one "can we also" at a time](/letters/scope-creep/) in a ticket thread.

That question turned into Builder Kickoff. Everyone in one room. Agree up front how many weeks this is worth, and let that number decide the scope.

You still get friction. It's the useful kind. The kind that happens before anyone's attached to a mockup.

The first time I ran it as a proper 90-minute session, I designed the full feature end to end the next day. Presented it to everyone the day after. PMs and engineers included. Zero pushback.

That's the extreme case. On most projects it just kills a ton of the friction you'd otherwise eat later. (The agenda is [here](/letters/project-kickoff-meeting/) if you want to steal it.)

But the kickoff was only the start. The part that kept bugging me was the end.

## "We'll ship and learn"

You've heard it. Let's just ship it and learn.

I'm not against that. Build fast, launch, iterate. Great.

But here's what actually happens. The iteration cycle is so long it never comes. The feature ships, everyone moves on, and the learning part quietly dies. Sixty days later somebody asks what it did, and the honest answer is "no idea, but people seemed to like it."

That's not learning. That's a vibe.

And it's exactly how design's value stays invisible. You did the work and you have zero receipts. Good luck putting "people seemed to like it" in a [case study](/letters/ux-case-study-template/).

So when someone says "ship and learn," I ask one question. Is learning actually part of our process? Are we looking at the [analytics](/letters/ux-metrics/)? Talking to sales? Going back to users?

Or are we just saying it to get the damn thing out the door?

## The loop

This is what I run now, start to finish. I call it the Builder Loop.

1. **Find the target.** One appetite, three non-goals, one decision sentence: we will ship X for Y by Z, without A, B or C.
2. **Write it down.** A [decision brief](/letters/product-brief-template/) everyone builds against, so the target doesn't drift one "quick question" at a time.
3. **Prove it's worth hitting.** Two real insights with a source, and the number you expect to move. Written down before design starts.
4. **Build toward it.** A working prototype in five days, checked against the decision sentence every single day.
5. **Check your aim.** A quality pass before handoff, and visual QA before you call it done.
6. **Did you hit it?** Sixty days after real customers can use it, predicted next to actual. Then one call: keep, iterate, investigate or stop.

Stage six feeds the next stage one. What you learned becomes the next target. That's why it's a loop.

Shipping is the halfway point. Most teams stop there and call it a win.
